The amount of retracted scientific papers has increased greatly over recent years, though it’s unclear whether it’s caused by an increase in flawed papers or better detection. To this end, we analyzed 2047 retracted articles in PubMed. The average retraction time was 32.91 months. Before 2002, articles took longer to retract (49.82 months) than articles post-2002 (23.82 months). For assessing increased scrutiny, we examined the relationship between time-to-retraction and journal impact factor. Impact factor only explained about 1% of the variance in retraction time – a relatively small contributing factor. Causes for retractions also changed. Once-rare plagiarism and duplicate publications have become more common. Plagiarism retractions now account for 9.4% of all retractions, which is still less than those for fraud or error. Additionally, retractions are more likely to occur earlier in the article’s life cycle, with a decline in time-to-retraction for articles published since 2000. This suggests fewer barriers to retraction.
Another factor for increasing retractions is the behavior of authors with a single retraction. From 1993 to 2012, 63.1% of retracted papers were written by authors with only one retraction, compared to 46% from 1972 to 1992 (p < 0.001). This suggests that retractions are now more broadly distributed across the scientific community rather than a few individuals.
In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects changes in the behavior of both authors and institutions. A combination of lower barrier to both publications and retractions are largely to blame. These findings demonstrate the importance of vigilance in maintaining scientific integrity.
